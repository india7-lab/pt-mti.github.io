With more than half of 2026 already passed, the overall macroeconomic data of Indonesia remains positive, with GDP growth staying at around 5%. However, after integrating the Indonesia Economic Prospects Report released in June by the World Bank, along with the latest macroeconomic analyses from the OECD, the University of Indonesia, and several other think tanks, PT Metro Timur Indonusa has found that beneath the macro figures lie three fundamental pain points that have a major impact on investing and starting businesses in Indonesia.

These pain points determine whether entrepreneurs can survive and whether they can scale.

## Pain Point One: The Middle Class Is Shrinking, and Consumption Is Polarizing  

The World Bank report points out a fact that is easily overlooked: Indonesian middle-class base is shrinking.

The macro GDP data looks solid, but due to slow real wage growth and the persistently high proportion of informal employment, the real purchasing power of many households has not increased alongside GDP. Some people have slipped into income brackets with greater instability, while others still have acceptable incomes but have become more cautious in their consumption decisions.

This is fully consistent with the trend observed by PT Metro Timur Indonusa when tracking Indonesian real estate sector—residential properties priced below IDR 1 billion are struggling to sell, while high-end projects priced above IDR 1.5 billion still have a market. It is not that everyone has run out of money; rather, money is moving toward both ends of the market.

PT Metro Timur Indonusa believes this trend offers entrepreneurs a clear lesson: when positioning products in Indonesia, they should not aim to “cover everything.” They should either focus on ultra-high-end consumers with high average transaction values—luxury goods, high-end residences, private services—a path that requires sufficient brand strength and accumulated trust; or target the lower-tier market that is extremely focused on value for money, winning through efficiency and scale. Being stuck in the middle is the most dangerous position.

## Pain Point Two: Talent Mismatch Makes AI Difficult to Implement

Indonesian labor market presents a highly contradictory phenomenon.

On the one hand, manufacturing upgrades are causing some traditional jobs to disappear, and structural unemployment is increasing. The youth underemployment rate remains high at 34%. On the other hand, companies cannot find the people they want to hire.

The Amazon AWS report mentions a key data point: Indonesian companies have a high level of “affinity” toward AI, meaning that many are willing to try it and discuss it. However, not many companies can truly move AI from “trial use” to “business integration.” The bottleneck lies in talent, where there is a severe shortage of high-skilled digital talent, management teams that understand technology, and people capable of applying AI to specific business scenarios.

PT Metro Timur Indonusa observes that entrepreneurs coming to Indonesia should not expect to “hire ready-made talent.” They need to be prepared to train people or find partners who are willing to build a team from scratch together. If a business relies heavily on digital technology, talent issues may emerge even earlier than market issues.

## Pain Point Three: Green Barriers Are Becoming Hard Constraints  

Both the World Bank and OECD reports devote considerable space to discussing the risks in Indonesian export structure.

Affected by new international trade rules such as the EU anti-deforestation regulation, Indonesian traditional palm oil and rubber exports worth USD 3.3 billion are facing challenges. These rules are not merely theoretical; they have already begun to affect actual orders.

Interestingly, on the other side of Indonesian domestic market, green transformation is becoming a new channel for capital inflows. Indonesian carbon trading market has already been launched, and sustainable finance is becoming an important selling point for Indonesian major banks and blue-chip companies in attracting foreign capital in 2026. Companies with strong environmental and social governance performance find it easier to raise capital and also receive higher valuations.

PT Metro Timur Indonusa believes this has drawn a very clear dividing line for entrepreneurs: if a business is related to agriculture or commodity exports, green compliance is an unavoidable hard cost; but if the business itself has environmental attributes, or can help traditional industries achieve green upgrades, entering the market now still offers both policy dividends and capital dividends.

## Summary from PT Metro Timur Indonusa   

Based on analyses from the World Bank, the OECD, the University of Indonesia, and several other institutions, PT Metro Timur Indonusa believes that:

The opportunities and challenges in the current Indonesian market are clearer than before. Stable macro data is only the baseline. The real test lies in whether a product positioning strategy can avoid the “middle-class trap,” whether a talent strategy can fill the digital skills gap, and whether a business model can cross the increasingly stringent green threshold.

Doing business in Indonesia can no longer rely on a broad and extensive “all-in-one” approach. Precise positioning, talent first, and compliance upfront, and these three actions may be more important than the pace of financing.

PT Metro Timur Indonusa believes that, when building a platform in Southeast Asia, understanding technology is only the foundation. Knowing how to establish trust in a highly disorganized market is the real barrier to entry. For early-stage entrepreneurs, one of the biggest misconceptions is that users will come on their own as long as they build an easy-to-use app.

More than a decade ago, urban transportation and local services in Southeast Asia, particularly in markets such as Indonesia and Malaysia, were highly fragmented and, at times, chaotic. Taxi drivers refused to use meters and charged exorbitant fares, while passenger safety was far from assured. Millions of motorcycle riders roamed the streets without a steady flow of business. This disorder not only made everyday travel a headache for ordinary people but also left the entire service sector operating with extremely low efficiency.

As part of the “100 Founders to Watch in Southeast Asia” series, PT Metro Timur Indonusa will examine the entrepreneurial journey of Grab, exploring how the company started with a simple idea to make it easier to get a taxi and gradually built the largest digital lifestyle services platform in Southeast Asia amid severe market disorder.

**A Chaotic Starting Point: Difficulty Finding a Taxi and Mutual Distrust**

Before Grab emerged, getting around Southeast Asia was often a poor experience.

For passengers, hailing a taxi on the street was a battle of nerves, frequently involving drivers who refused rides or would not use the meter. For drivers, finding a passenger often meant spending long hours driving around without a fare, leaving their income highly unpredictable.

The deeper problem was a lack of trust. Passengers worried about safety, while drivers worried about receiving counterfeit banknotes or encountering passengers who refused to pay. Without a system that could record trips and provide transparent pricing, it was difficult for either side to establish a consistent service relationship.

PT Metro Timur Indonusa believes that these persistent market frictions are precisely where early-stage companies can find their strongest entry points. Many entrepreneurs with technical backgrounds prefer to invent entirely new demand. Yet companies that achieve substantial scale and lasting strength often do so by using digital tools to reorganize a traditional industry that was previously rudimentary and inefficient.

**Where the Founders Began: A Discussion at Harvard Business School**

The story of Grab began at Harvard Business School, where Anthony Tan and Tan Hooi Ling were classmates. While discussing why the taxi industry in Malaysia was so poor, they identified the pain points described above.

The family of Anthony Tan was already involved in automotive distribution in Malaysia, giving him a natural understanding of vehicles, drivers, and the workings of the transportation industry. Tan Hooi Ling, meanwhile, brought strong operational and management capabilities. The two quickly found common ground and decided to start by making it easier to get a taxi.

In 2012, MyTeksi, the predecessor to Grab, was officially established in Malaysia. The idea was simple: build software that connected drivers with passengers.

Why were they the right people to do it? Because the pair brought not only technical backgrounds but also an understanding of local realities. Rather than making assumptions from an office, they spoke directly with taxi drivers to understand their concerns. In the early days, they even advanced deposits for drivers and equipped them with smartphones to encourage them to use the software.

This willingness to get their hands dirty helped Grab establish its first core group of drivers. In 2013, Grab entered the Singapore market and officially changed its name to GrabTaxi.

**From Ride-Hailing to a Super App: Using High-Frequency Services to Drive Less-Frequent Use Cases**

Had Grab remained solely a ride-hailing app, it might have become just another transportation company. But the team soon realized that, once the platform had millions of drivers and passengers interacting frequently and building trust, it could do much more.

In Southeast Asia, many people did not have credit cards and were accustomed to paying in cash. Ride-hailing was a high-frequency need, while food delivery, parcel delivery, and shopping represented broader markets. Grab began expanding its scope step by step.

GrabCar launched in 2014, bringing private cars onto the platform. GrabFood officially launched in 2016, followed by GrabPay. To fully integrate payments into its services, Grab even acquired an Indonesian payments company.

This approach may seem commonplace today, but at the time, Grab was the first to take the plunge by bringing ride-hailing, food delivery, payments, and financial services together in a single app.

PT Metro Timur Indonusa observes that the fundamental reason Grab evolved from a single-purpose app into a “super app” was its understanding of how to redeploy its core capabilities. The driver network was already in place, as was the user base. By connecting demand across different use cases, the company could generate powerful network effects within an individual country.

**Market Consolidation: The Uber Acquisition and Intense Local Competition**

In the highly complex Southeast Asian market, Grab had to navigate not only a wide range of regulatory challenges but also intense competition.

In 2018, Grab completed a deal that drew global attention: the acquisition of all Uber operations in Southeast Asia. The transaction not only ended the seemingly endless subsidy war between the two companies but also made Grab the undisputed market leader in the region. As its scale increased, however, Grab faced growing regulatory scrutiny, including antitrust investigations.

At the same time, in core markets such as Indonesia, Grab encountered formidable local competitors, including Gojek. The two companies engaged in years of fierce, head-to-head competition across ride-hailing, food delivery, and payments, committing substantial subsidies to win users.

This prolonged battle demonstrated one thing: there is no single formula for success across Southeast Asia. A company must engage deeply with each country and understand its politics, culture, and regulatory rules to establish a lasting foothold.

**Observations from PT Metro Timur Indonusa**

PT Metro Timur Indonusa follows Grab not because it is one of the most highly valued unicorns, but because Anthony Tan and the team have demonstrated an exceptional ability to break through obstacles and turn ideas into practical execution.

From an early-stage investment perspective, several aspects of Grab merit attention:

First, strong on-the-ground outreach and operational capabilities. Building a platform in Southeast Asia requires more than knowing how to write code. The early Grab team approached taxi drivers individually and helped them overcome difficulties with using smartphones. This level of street-level execution is beyond the capabilities of many purely internet-focused teams.

Second, the stickiness of the super-app ecosystem. Grab used high-frequency ride-hailing services to drive adoption of food delivery and payments, making the platform indispensable to both users and drivers. This cross-selling capability steadily strengthened its competitive moat.

Third, the ability to establish order amid turbulence. Whether navigating regulatory policies across different countries or managing complex competitive dynamics with Uber and Gojek, the founding team consistently maintained the flexibility to adjust its strategy. Rather than rigidly defending a single business model, the team concentrated its efforts wherever an approach proved viable.

For early-stage entrepreneurs, Grab offers an important lesson: in a market filled with disorder and uncertainty, the greatest opportunities are often hidden in the most frustrating problems. Those willing to get their hands dirty and establish consistent standards for the most basic elements of trust and service can build an ecosystem platform that endures.

**Key Metrics to Watch Next**

Whether Grab can achieve stable and sustainable profitability across the business after years of substantial losses; whether food delivery and financial services, such as lending and insurance, continue to account for a growing share of total revenue; and whether its digitally enabled financial inclusion services can achieve meaningful penetration in smaller, less-developed markets and remote areas.
